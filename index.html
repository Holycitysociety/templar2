About the Restoration

On that day—precisely seven hundred and seventeen years and eight months after the unlawful silencing of the Order—the archetypal vow of Christian chivalry was once again spoken before God and the Church. This vow, unaltered in its essence since the twelfth century, was taken in verbo Domini as both a spiritual consecration and a juridical act, fulfilling the criteria of perpetual profession outlined in Gratian’s Decretum (D. 93 c. 24 Nullus sacerdos) and the Decretals of Gregory IX (I.2.3 De officio et potestate Papae).

Canonical and Historical Proof of Continuity

The canonical defense of this restoration rests on three principal defects in the 1312 suppression of the Order by Pope Clement V, articulated in the bull Vox in Excelso:

I. Defectus Causae — Defect of Cause

The Chinon Parchment (A.D. 1308) issued under papal authority absolved the Templar leadership from all charges of heresy and reconciled them to the Church. Consequently, there existed no canonical cause for suppression. A papal act of dissolution that contradicts a prior absolution is ipso iure null according to Gratian (C.11 q.3 c.40 Si Papa).

II. Defectus Formae — Defect of Form

The bull Vox in Excelso was promulgated as an administrative decree, not as a judicial sentence. It lacked the formal trial required by canonical due process as established in Decretales Gregorii IX (X 5.39.35 Ad abolendam). Thus, it cannot constitute a lawful suppression [oai_citation:3‡GAB AI CANNONICAL DEFENSE.pdf](file-service://file_00000000122c620c8dd56a5eab307a5c).

III. Defectus Libertatis — Defect of Free Will

Pope Clement V acted under duress from King Philip IV, whose army encircled the papal residence. Canon law invalidates any juridical act performed under coercion (C.15 q.6 c.3 Non debet). Therefore, the suppression was issued non ex voluntate sed ex metu—not by free will but by fear—and was canonically void.

Given these cumulative defects, the suppression lacked legal effect and could not extinguish the Order’s juridical personality, its Rule, or its papal privileges. The Templar charism therefore remained in abeyance, not abolished.

Frequently Raised Objections and Canonical Responses

1. “Doesn’t only the Pope have authority to restore an order?”

Correct—yet when a suppression was itself canonically void, no new papal act is required for restoration. The principle of vi consuetudinis immemorabilis allows lawful reactivation of an unextinguished institution when its original rights were perpetual and divinely oriented (Regulae Iuris 6; Decretum Gratiani D.1 c.7).

2. “Isn’t this disobedient to the Church?”

No. The act explicitly professes obedience to the Roman Pontiff and asserts no jurisdiction contrary to him. It proceeds praeter legem sed non contra legem, reviving what was never lawfully removed, much like the revival of suppressed monastic congregations under canon 215 and 299 §1-2 of the 1983 Code.

3. “Couldn’t this be seen as a new order instead of the original?”

The restoration invokes potestas propria (inherent authority) and immemorial custom, making it juridically continuous with the original body. In canon law, a juridical person that was never lawfully suppressed retains its moral existence until formally recognized again (Codex Iuris Canonici 1983, can. 120 §1).

4. “Why is the Chinon Parchment central?”

Because it proves Pope Clement V absolved the Templars and reconciled them with the Church in 1308. Suppression following absolution without cause violates the canonical maxim absolutio tollit causam—“absolution removes the case.”

5. “Why after 717 years?”

The passage of time does not extinguish rights conferred in perpetuum by papal bull. Immemorial custom operates precisely in such long durations, where human lapse does not negate divine or apostolic grant.
